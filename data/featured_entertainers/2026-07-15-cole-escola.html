FEATURED ENTERTAINER OF THE WEEK

Cole Escola

Creator and performer

July 15, 2026

Oh, Mary!, Starring Cole Escola, to Be Filmed in London for Upcoming Release

Cole Escola has turned a joke that should not work into one of the most unlikely theatrical success stories of the decade. Oh, Mary! is not a sober historical drama about Mary Todd Lincoln, nor is it a careful period comedy with museum-glass respect for the 1860s. It is a delirious, tightly built farce about a furious, boozy, fame-starved Mary who wants to be a cabaret star while her husband is busy with the Civil War. In lesser hands, that premise might have lasted three minutes in a sketch show. Escola made it into a stage phenomenon. This week, the reason is especially clear: Escola will star as Mary Todd Lincoln in a filmed version of Oh, Mary!, captured at London’s Trafalgar Theatre on July 28 for an upcoming release. That matters because the performance has become one of those rare live-theatre events people talk about as if they saw a comet. The filmed version will preserve a creation that began in downtown comedy, conquered Off-Broadway, moved to Broadway, crossed the Atlantic and became an Olivier Award-winning comedy. Escola’s rise is not the familiar Hollywood arc of a performer waiting for the right executive to notice them. It is stranger and more self-made. They built a comic language from old television, melodrama, queer performance, fake glamour, bad wigs, and characters who seem ridiculous until they reveal exactly how lonely, vain, frightened or hungry they are. Before Mary Todd Lincoln became a prestige-stage monster, Escola was already a cult figure to comedy devotees: a performer who could make a character feel both absurdly artificial and psychologically precise. Oh, Mary! did not come from nowhere. It is the result of years spent refining a style that looked too odd for the mainstream until the mainstream finally caught up.

Early Life

Cole Escola grew up in Clatskanie, a small town in Oregon, far from the New York comedy rooms and Broadway theatres that would later shape their career. That distance is part of the story. Escola has often seemed like a performer who absorbed American entertainment from the outside in: old sitcom rhythms, movie-star poses, daytime-television sincerity, vintage melodrama, children’s-show brightness, and the peculiar emotional weather of people trying very hard to be seen. They moved to New York as a young adult and studied at Marymount Manhattan College before making their way into the city’s alternative comedy and queer performance scenes. Those rooms rewarded specificity. A joke did not have to be broad if the character was sharp enough; a wig could be cheap if the point of view was expensive. Escola’s early work developed in that spirit. Rather than chase a conventional stand-up persona, they created figures who felt as if they had walked out of half-remembered television broadcasts and immediately started confessing. That background explains why Oh, Mary! feels simultaneously silly and exact. Escola’s comedy is rarely just about the punchline. It is about the pose before the punchline, the hunger behind the pose, and the moment when the pose collapses.

Career

Escola first attracted attention in the late 2000s through a route that now feels prophetic: self-made online comedy and downtown performance. With Jeffery Self, they created Jeffery & Cole Casserole, a surreal comedy project that began as a web series before moving to Logo. It was low-budget, strange, knowingly awkward and filled with the kind of comic timing that suggested Escola did not need a large platform to make an impression. They could make a glance, a pause or a badly behaved character feel like an event. That early visibility placed Escola in a lineage of performers who used the internet not as a shortcut to celebrity but as a workshop. The audience was smaller, but the voice was unmistakable. Television eventually found them, though not always in obvious leading-role form. Escola became one of those performers who could enter a scene and tilt its temperature. On Difficult People, they played Matthew, adding a coolly odd presence to a comedy already built around sharp mouths and show-business neurosis. On Search Party, they appeared as Chip Wreck, a character who let Escola show how easily comedy can curdle into menace. The role was funny because it was unsettling, not despite it. Animation also suited them. In Big Mouth and Human Resources, they voiced Montel, bringing a bright, mischievous weirdness to shows already stocked with extreme personalities. The most important television collaboration, however, was At Home with Amy Sedaris. Escola co-created, wrote for, produced and appeared in the series, which reimagined the homemaking show as a cracked comedy cabinet. The series understood something that also runs through Escola’s own work: domestic cheer can be frightening, hospitality can become performance art, and a perfectly arranged craft table can hide chaos. Working with Amy Sedaris, a master of cheerful grotesquerie, gave Escola a larger canvas for a comic sensibility that had long mixed sweetness with derangement. The show’s Emmy recognition mattered because it confirmed that this strain of handmade, character-driven absurdism could exist inside the awards conversation without becoming bland. Still, nothing changed Escola’s public profile like Oh, Mary!. The play began Off-Broadway at the Lucille Lortel Theatre and quickly became a word-of-mouth event. The title character was not the solemn Mary Todd Lincoln of schoolbooks. Escola’s Mary was a catastrophic diva, a woman with no usable political awareness and an almost religious belief in her own thwarted stage destiny. The audacity was not only in the premise but in the discipline. Oh, Mary! is tightly constructed farce, not random historical vandalism. The jokes land because the play commits to its own ridiculous rules. Sam Pinkleton’s direction sharpened the velocity, while a cast including Conrad Ricamora, James Scully and Bianca Leigh helped build a world in which every character was trapped inside Mary’s gravitational pull. The Broadway transfer to the Lyceum Theatre transformed Escola from cult comedy favorite into a mainstream theatrical name. Awards followed, including major recognition from the Tony Awards. That recognition mattered beyond a trophy count. It signaled that a performer whose work had long thrived in queer, alternative and internet-native spaces could reshape commercial theatre without sanding off the oddness. Escola’s Tony win for leading actor in a play placed them among the small number of nonbinary performers to receive one of theatre’s highest acting honors, and it made Oh, Mary! part of a broader conversation about who gets to be centered on major stages. The show’s later West End life and Olivier success only extended that argument. What once sounded like a dare became an international theatrical property. The filmed London performance now brings the story to another turning point. Theatre is famously temporary; legendary comic performances often survive only in memories and rave reviews. Capturing Escola’s Mary at Trafalgar Theatre means audiences outside New York and London will be able to see the timing, physicality and vocal precision that turned a deranged historical fantasy into a career-defining work. It also places Escola in a tradition of stage performers whose signature creations become cultural documents. For someone who started by building comedy in small rooms and online corners, that is a remarkable full-circle moment.

Interesting Facts

  • Oh, Mary! is deliberately not a historically faithful portrait of Mary Todd Lincoln; its comedy comes from treating a wildly inaccurate theatrical fantasy with total conviction.
  • The comma in Oh, Mary! is part of the title, giving it the melodramatic snap of an exasperated address.
  • Escola built a following before mainstream awards attention through online sketches and New York alternative comedy rooms.
  • Jeffery & Cole Casserole began as a web project before becoming a television series on Logo, making it an early example of internet comedy crossing into cable.
  • At Home with Amy Sedaris paired Escola with Amy Sedaris, one of the performers most closely associated with cheerful, handmade absurdism.
  • Escola’s Search Party character Chip Wreck showed how their comic style could become genuinely unnerving without losing its humor.
  • Their comedy often draws from older television formats, including homemaking shows, melodrama, sitcoms and children’s programming.
  • Oh, Mary! made the Lyceum Theatre, one of Broadway’s oldest operating theatres, feel like the home of a thoroughly contemporary queer farce.
  • The Broadway success of Oh, Mary! was unusual because it was driven by a star turn from its own playwright, not by a pre-existing movie title or jukebox score.
  • Escola’s Tony recognition placed them among the small group of nonbinary performers to receive major acting honors on Broadway.
  • The filmed London version is significant because Escola’s Mary Todd Lincoln is a highly physical comic performance, dependent on timing, posture, facial control and vocal attack.
  • Escola’s work often makes artificiality the point: the wig, the accent, the theatrical gesture and the visible performance become part of the character’s psychology.

Why ShowBiz Selected This Entertainer

Cole Escola is featured this week because Oh, Mary! is moving from theatrical phenomenon toward screen preservation. The planned filming at London’s Trafalgar Theatre gives a wider audience access to a performance that has already reshaped Escola’s career and become one of the defining comic stage turns of the moment. The news is not simply about distribution; it is about permanence. For years, Escola’s appeal lived in rooms, clips, cult television appearances and the memories of audiences who understood that their characters were funnier because they were so specifically wounded and strange. Oh, Mary! brought that sensibility to a far larger stage without diluting it. Its Broadway success, Tony recognition and Olivier-winning London life connect Escola’s downtown comedy roots to international theatre acclaim. The filmed version now marks the next chapter: a once-ephemeral performance becoming part of the permanent record.
