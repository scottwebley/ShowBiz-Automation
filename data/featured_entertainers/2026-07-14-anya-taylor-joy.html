Anya Taylor-Joy

Anya Taylor-Joy Turns Another Risk Into the Main Event

Anya Taylor-Joy has built a career out of looking impossible to predict. She can enter a scene like a porcelain apparition, a chess prodigy, a desert warrior, a Jane Austen heroine or a nightclub singer from a half-remembered dream, and somehow the performance is never just about the surface. The face may be instantly recognizable; the choices are what keep shifting. That is why her arrival in Lucky, Apple TV's seven-part limited crime thriller, makes sense as this week's defining entertainment moment. The first two episodes have begun streaming, with new chapters rolling out weekly through August 19, and the timing finds Taylor-Joy in a particularly interesting phase: no longer the discovery from The Witch, no longer merely the breakout star of The Queen's Gambit, but an actress now treated as an event in herself. Reviews and industry conversation around Lucky have arrived alongside fresh attention on her expected return as Alia Atreides in Dune: Part Three, placing her at the center of both prestige television and one of cinema's biggest science-fiction franchises. For Taylor-Joy, that combination is not unusual. Her career has rarely moved in a straight line. She became famous through horror, became a global name through chess, gave Jane Austen a mischievous modern pulse, stepped into George Miller's wasteland with the pressure of following Charlize Theron, and still found room to voice Princess Peach in a billion-dollar animated hit. The roles can look wildly different on paper, but they share one thing: Taylor-Joy is often cast at the point where innocence, intelligence and danger meet. Lucky is the latest reminder that her specialty is not transformation for its own sake. It is tension.

Early Life

Anya Josephine Marie Taylor-Joy was born in Miami, but her childhood belongs to more than one map. She spent her earliest years in Buenos Aires, speaking Spanish before English and absorbing Argentine culture before her family moved to London when she was six. The relocation was not easy. Taylor-Joy has spoken in interviews about resisting English at first because she believed refusing the language might persuade her family to return to Argentina. That early displacement became part of the emotional machinery of her work. Many of her characters exist between worlds: Thomasin in The Witch is trapped between childhood and accusation; Beth Harmon in The Queen's Gambit is suspended between genius and isolation; Furiosa is defined by a stolen home; Alia Atreides is born into prophecy before she can choose ordinary life. Taylor-Joy's performances often carry that sense of a person listening for a language only they can hear. She attended schools including Hill House and Queen's Gate in London and trained in dance when she was younger, especially ballet. As a teenager, she was spotted by Sarah Doukas, the founder of Storm Management, the same agency figure associated with the discovery of Kate Moss. Modeling gave Taylor-Joy a route into the industry, but acting was the destination. She left school at 16 and pursued auditions with the conviction of someone who did not want a conventional path badly enough to pretend otherwise.

Career

Taylor-Joy's screen career began modestly, with early television work and a small part in Vampire Academy that was ultimately cut. In another career, that might have been a discouraging footnote. In hers, it became part of the rhythm: near-misses, strange openings, and then a role so precisely suited to her that the industry had to pay attention. That role was Thomasin in Robert Eggers' The Witch. Released in 2015 after making a stir at Sundance, the film did not introduce Taylor-Joy as a conventional ingénue. It placed her in a severe, historically meticulous New England nightmare, speaking period dialogue and carrying a story about faith, repression and female suspicion. Horror often produces major acting discoveries, but The Witch did something more specific for Taylor-Joy. It announced that she could hold silence without emptiness. Her stillness had voltage. Eggers' film became a calling card for both director and star, and Taylor-Joy was quickly marked as an actress who could make genre material feel literary. Hollywood responded by testing that presence in different registers. M. Night Shyamalan's Split put her opposite James McAvoy in a commercially successful thriller and introduced her to a wider audience as Casey Cooke, a survivor whose watchfulness concealed trauma and resolve. The film was a box-office breakthrough and later connected to Glass, placing Taylor-Joy inside Shyamalan's Unbreakable universe. Around the same period, Thoroughbreds paired her with Olivia Cooke in a dry, unsettling tale of privilege and moral rot. It remains one of the sharpest early examples of Taylor-Joy's ability to play characters whose composure feels like a locked room. The next major turn came from an unexpected direction: Jane Austen. In Emma., Autumn de Wilde's bright, stylized 2020 adaptation, Taylor-Joy played Emma Woodhouse not as a sweet heroine waiting to be corrected, but as a clever, vain, emotionally immature young woman learning the consequences of her own cleverness. The performance mattered because it expanded the public idea of what Taylor-Joy could do. She had been praised for intensity; Emma. let her be funny, imperious, romantic and ridiculous without losing elegance. A Golden Globe nomination followed, and the film became an important bridge between cult admiration and mainstream prestige. Then came The Queen's Gambit. Released by Netflix in 2020, the limited series became a global phenomenon at a moment when much of the world was still living through pandemic confinement. As Beth Harmon, Taylor-Joy gave viewers a genius they could root for without simplifying her. She communicated calculation, addiction, loneliness, competitiveness and glamour with a performance that made chess cinematic without pretending the board itself was enough. The series won widespread acclaim and became one of Netflix's defining limited-series successes. For Taylor-Joy, it brought the Golden Globe, SAG Award and Critics Choice Award, along with an Emmy nomination. More importantly, it changed her scale. She was no longer merely a promising actress with impeccable taste. She was a star who could carry a global hit. What followed could have been a predictable move into safe leading roles. Taylor-Joy instead kept complicating the picture. Edgar Wright's Last Night in Soho used her as an image of 1960s glamour curdling into horror; she also performed the song Downtown for the film, reminding audiences that her screen presence could extend into music when the role demanded it. Robert Eggers brought her into The Northman, a brutal Viking epic, where she played Olga of the Birch Forest opposite Alexander Skarsgard. In The Menu, she became the skeptical outsider at a deadly temple of luxury dining, holding her own against Ralph Fiennes in a satire that turned class performance into a horror show. At the same time, Taylor-Joy entered global franchise territory without fully surrendering to it. She voiced Princess Peach in The Super Mario Bros. Movie, a commercial smash that introduced her voice to a family audience on a massive scale. Denis Villeneuve's Dune: Part Two brought her into the Dune universe as Alia Atreides, a role with enormous significance in Frank Herbert's mythology. Even before Dune: Part Three arrives, the casting has generated fascination because Alia is not a decorative figure; she is one of the saga's most complicated presences, bound to questions of inheritance, power and destiny. The most demanding test of Taylor-Joy's post-Queen's Gambit stardom may have been Furiosa: A Mad Max Saga. Taking over a character indelibly associated with Charlize Theron's performance in Mad Max: Fury Road was not a simple succession. George Miller chose to tell a younger Furiosa story rather than digitally recreate the previous star, placing Taylor-Joy in a role defined by physical endurance, minimal dialogue and mythic intensity. The film asked her to project a lifetime of rage and survival through restraint, especially in long stretches where the character speaks little. Whether measured as action cinema, prequel storytelling or star challenge, Furiosa showed Taylor-Joy willing to step into a dangerous comparison and make the character's younger incarnation her own. Lucky now arrives at a moment when Taylor-Joy's career has acquired rare flexibility. She can headline television, lead auteur horror, appear in literary adaptations, join billion-dollar intellectual property and still be spoken about as a performer rather than simply a celebrity. Her fame has grown, but the interesting thing is how often she continues to choose roles built on unease. She seems less interested in being liked than in being watched closely.

Why ShowBiz Selected This Entertainer

Anya Taylor-Joy is ShowBiz.com's Featured Entertainer this week because Lucky has put her back at the center of the television conversation. The Apple TV limited crime thriller premiered with its first two episodes now streaming and a weekly rollout continuing through August 19, bringing a fresh round of reviews and attention to one of the most closely watched actresses of her generation. The timing matters. Lucky is not an isolated star vehicle; it arrives as Taylor-Joy's career is expanding in several directions at once. She is headlining limited-series television after the enormous impact of The Queen's Gambit, producing as well as acting, and simultaneously drawing interest for her return as Alia Atreides in Dune: Part Three. That combination makes her an unusually revealing figure in the current entertainment landscape: an actress who can move between streaming prestige, auteur cinema and global franchise storytelling without being defined by only one of them. This week's news is the hook, but the larger story is staying power. Taylor-Joy became famous by choosing roles with risk built into them. Lucky continues that pattern.
