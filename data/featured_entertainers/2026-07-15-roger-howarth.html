FEATURED ENTERTAINER OF THE WEEK

Roger Howarth

Actor

July 15, 2026

Roger Howarth Turns a Villain’s Return Into a Career-Reframing Emmy Moment

Roger Howarth has always been most dangerous on television when a soap opera thinks it knows exactly what to do with him. Cast him as a predator, and he finds the fracture lines that make audiences recoil and keep watching. Hand him a romantic antihero, and he plays the charm as if it might curdle at any second. Bring him back after a long absence, and he makes the return feel less like nostalgia than unfinished business. That is why his 2026 Daytime Emmy nomination for Outstanding Supporting Actor for playing Matt Clark on The Young and the Restless feels larger than a standard awards-season footnote. It is Howarth’s first Daytime Emmy nomination in 21 years, and it arrives after a career spent proving that daytime television can hold some of American drama’s strangest, riskiest and most durable performances. For viewers who came to him through One Life to Live, he remains Todd Manning, one of the most complicated creations in modern soap history. For As the World Turns loyalists, he is Paul Ryan, a damaged scion whose worst choices never quite erased his vulnerability. For General Hospital fans, he is the rare actor who survived not one but three identity resets on the same show: Todd, Franco Baldwin and Austin Gatlin-Holt. Now, on The Young and the Restless, his work as Matt Clark has given the industry a reason to look again at an actor whose gift has never been ease. Howarth’s specialty is discomfort, and in daytime, discomfort often becomes electricity.

Early Life

Roger Howarth came out of New York’s theatrical orbit rather than the glossy pipeline that later fed so many daytime breakouts. Raised in Hastings-on-Hudson, north of New York City, he grew up close enough to the city’s stages for acting to seem like a profession rather than a fantasy, yet far enough away to develop the wary, observant quality that would become one of his signatures on screen. He did not arrive in daytime as a conventional leading man with a perfectly polished persona. What made him stand out was something more elusive: an ability to seem intelligent, wounded, defensive and unpredictable all at once. Early in his career, that quality made him a natural fit for soaps, a format that rewards actors who can make an impossible plot feel emotionally immediate by the end of a single scene. Daytime television has long been a training ground for performers with stamina, memory and nerve. Howarth had all three. The pace was relentless, the emotional turns were extreme, and the camera was close enough to expose a false note. Rather than sanding down his odd edges, he built a career around them.

Career

Roger Howarth’s career is inseparable from Todd Manning, but reducing him to that role misses the more interesting story. Todd was introduced on One Life to Live in the early 1990s as part of one of the show’s most controversial storylines: the rape of Marty Saybrooke. In a weaker performance, Todd could have remained a one-note villain, useful only as a dramatic catalyst before being written away. Howarth did something more unsettling. He did not soften the crime, but he played Todd as a man whose arrogance, self-loathing and hunger for control were constantly fighting inside him. Audiences were repulsed by Todd and fascinated by him. That tension helped make the character one of the defining figures of 1990s daytime drama. It also placed Howarth in a complicated position. Todd’s popularity forced the genre to confront one of its recurring ethical dilemmas: what happens when a charismatic actor makes a reprehensible character dramatically irresistible? Howarth’s Emmy win in 1994 for Outstanding Younger Actor was not merely a personal breakthrough. It signaled that daytime could reward a performance built on moral instability rather than easy likability. The role also came with baggage. As Todd evolved into a central figure and, eventually, a romantic lead, the character became a lightning rod for debate among fans and critics. Howarth’s discomfort with the direction of Todd’s redemption arc has been widely discussed over the years, and it became part of the mythology around both actor and character. That unease matters. It explains why his best work never feels as if it is asking the viewer to forgive too quickly. He tends to leave the bruise visible. After periods away from One Life to Live, Howarth moved to As the World Turns in 2003 as Paul Ryan, a role with its own deep history. Recasting in soaps is a brutal art: viewers bring memories of past actors, old storylines and private loyalties. Howarth did not try to erase the character’s history. He played Paul as a man who had inherited emotional wreckage and learned to weaponize it. The part gave him a different canvas from Todd. Paul was not defined by a single infamous act. He was a volatile son, lover and schemer whose damage accumulated across relationships. The work earned Howarth a Daytime Emmy nomination for Outstanding Lead Actor in 2005, confirming that his post-Todd career was not simply a second act but a serious continuation. He stayed with As the World Turns until the show’s final year, becoming part of the last generation of actors to carry one of daytime’s great legacy series before its cancellation in 2010. Then came one of the strangest chapters in modern soap casting. In 2011, Howarth returned to One Life to Live during the show’s final ABC stretch, entering a story built around the mystery of the real Todd Manning and the version played for years by Trevor St. John. It was soap opera as identity puzzle, meta-commentary and fan service all at once. When One Life to Live ended on ABC, Howarth’s Todd crossed to General Hospital, briefly giving ABC’s daytime universe the feeling of a shared dramatic ecosystem. But behind-the-scenes rights complications tied to the online revival of One Life to Live meant that General Hospital could not simply keep using certain characters as planned. Rather than lose Howarth, the show did something only soaps can do with a straight face: it brought him back as someone else. That someone else was Franco Baldwin, a character originally associated with James Franco’s high-profile guest appearances. On paper, the assignment looked nearly impossible. Franco had a bizarre, heavily stylized history, and the name carried celebrity baggage. Howarth’s version gradually became less stunt casting and more character rehabilitation. He made Franco sardonic, damaged and unexpectedly tender, particularly as the character’s relationships deepened. It was another example of his unusual daytime specialty: stepping into material that could have collapsed under its own past and finding a playable human pulse inside it. When Franco was killed off in 2021, General Hospital again kept Howarth in the fold, this time as Austin Gatlin-Holt, a doctor with ties to the Quartermaine family. Austin never became the cultural flashpoint that Todd had been or the long-running anchor that Franco became, but the role underlined something important about Howarth’s career. Producers repeatedly trusted him with difficult transitions. They knew audiences would follow his face into new narrative territory. His guest work outside soaps has often played off the same tension. On Dawson’s Creek, he appeared as Professor Greg Hetson, a bracingly adult presence in a teen drama that had grown into college-age self-examination. On The Flash, he played Mason Bridge, a journalist whose curiosity put him in danger. These were not career-defining roles, but they showed how well Howarth’s sharpness translates beyond daytime. He can suggest a whole interior life in a handful of scenes, which is one reason soap viewers are so protective of him. Daytime actors often do more with less time, less rehearsal and more narrative whiplash than their prime-time peers. Howarth’s work has been a decades-long argument for taking that skill seriously. The Young and the Restless nomination brings the story full circle in a surprising way. Matt Clark, like Todd Manning, belongs to the darker side of soap history. By stepping into that orbit, Howarth is not escaping his past; he is conversing with it. The Emmy nomination recognizes a performance, but it also acknowledges endurance. Twenty-one years between nominations is a lifetime in television. Entire networks have rebranded, soaps have vanished, streaming has rewritten viewing habits, and daytime has had to fight repeatedly for cultural relevance. Howarth has survived those shifts not by reinventing himself into something smooth and anonymous, but by remaining recognizably himself: intense, exacting, unpredictable and allergic to emotional shortcuts.

Why ShowBiz Selected This Entertainer

Roger Howarth is featured this week because his 2026 Daytime Emmy nomination for Outstanding Supporting Actor for The Young and the Restless marks his first Daytime Emmy recognition in 21 years. That gap gives the moment its weight. This is not simply a nomination for a current performance; it is a reminder of a career that has moved through some of daytime television’s most volatile terrain. Howarth has played characters that forced viewers to argue about redemption, accountability, identity and reinvention. He has survived the end of One Life to Live on ABC, the cancellation of As the World Turns, the legal and creative complications that reshaped his General Hospital tenure, and the constant churn of a genre often underestimated by the wider industry. His nomination for Matt Clark puts him back in the Emmy conversation while also inviting a fresh look at why his work has endured. Few actors in daytime have been asked to carry so much moral ambiguity for so long. Fewer still have made that ambiguity feel newly alive three decades later.
