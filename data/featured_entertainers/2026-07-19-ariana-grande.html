Ariana Grande: The Pop Star Who Made Glinda a Career-Defining Second Act

Ariana Grande has spent much of her public life being underestimated in one direction or another. On Nickelodeon, she was the tiny red-haired comic spark who could sing like a Broadway veteran. In pop, she became a streaming-era force so quickly that it was easy to miss how technically disciplined her voice had always been. Now, with Wicked, she has pulled off the rarer trick: returning to musical theater not as a celebrity visitor, but as someone who clearly belongs there. That is why Grande is ShowBiz.com’s featured entertainer this week. Her performance as Glinda in Wicked has placed her at the center of awards conversation, earning Golden Globe and Academy Award nominations and reminding audiences that her career did not begin with arena tours or chart records. It began onstage. Grande’s story is not simply a pop-star-goes-to-Hollywood arc. It is almost the reverse: a theater kid became a sitcom personality, then a global pop star, then circled back to the form that first trained her timing, breath control and appetite for big emotional gestures. The Wicked attention matters because it ties together pieces of her career that once looked separate: the Broadway discipline of 13, the heightened comedy of Victorious, the vocal athleticism of Yours Truly and Dangerous Woman, the public resilience after Manchester, and the more reflective artist who made eternal sunshine. Grande has often worked at a pace that would flatten a less prepared performer. Her best moments, however, have tended to come when she treats spectacle as precision work. In Wicked, she is not merely hitting notes. She is calibrating a character whose sweetness, vanity, ambition and loneliness all have to coexist. For an entertainer who became famous before she had fully grown up in public, that kind of control feels like the point of the whole journey.

Early Life

Ariana Grande-Butera grew up in Boca Raton, a South Florida setting far removed from the entertainment-industry machinery that later surrounded her. Her mother, Joan Grande, has run Hose-McCann Communications, a company connected to communications and safety equipment, while her father, Edward Butera, is a graphic designer and owner of a design firm. Grande’s childhood was not a case of an anonymous talent suddenly discovered by a record label. She was performing early, developing the habits of a stage child before most audiences knew her name. She appeared in local theater and sang in public settings, including with the Fort Lauderdale Children’s Theater, where she gained experience in musicals before her professional breakthrough. Her formative training is important because it explains much of what later seemed effortless. Grande’s pop vocals are often discussed in terms of range, especially her upper register, but theater gave her more than high notes. It gave her diction, timing, the ability to sell a lyric as a thought, and the stamina to repeat demanding material night after night. Her first major professional break came when she was cast in the Broadway musical 13, Jason Robert Brown’s coming-of-age show about adolescence, social status and the awkward politics of growing up. Grande was 15 when she appeared in the production, which also featured Elizabeth Gillies, later her Victorious co-star. It was not the kind of role that instantly turns a young actor into a household name, but it placed her inside a serious New York theater environment. In retrospect, that early Broadway credit looks less like a footnote and more like the foundation for everything that followed.

Career

Grande’s career has unfolded in distinct chapters, but the chapters speak to each other more than they first appear. The Broadway role in 13 led naturally to television auditions, and in 2010 she became Cat Valentine on Nickelodeon’s Victorious. Cat was spacey, sweet, unpredictable and delivered in a high, airy voice that became instantly recognizable to young viewers. For some performers, a successful children’s television role can become a cage. Grande’s challenge was that Cat Valentine was beloved, but the character’s exaggerated innocence risked obscuring the singer underneath. Yet Nickelodeon also gave her something invaluable: a daily education in camera comedy, ensemble rhythm and mass audience familiarity. Victorious ran for four seasons, and Grande continued the role in the spin-off Sam & Cat opposite Jennette McCurdy. By the time that series ended, she had built a large youth audience, but she had also made it clear she was aiming beyond sitcom fame. Her debut album, Yours Truly, arrived in 2013 and opened at No. 1 on the Billboard 200. The single The Way, featuring Mac Miller, introduced many pop listeners to the combination that would define her early sound: throwback R&B textures, a bright melodic sensibility and a voice comfortable with melisma, whistle tones and quick rhythmic turns. Comparisons to Mariah Carey followed immediately. They were flattering but limiting. Grande would spend the next decade proving that influence is not the same as imitation. My Everything in 2014 turned her into a full-scale pop presence, with Problem, Break Free, Bang Bang and Love Me Harder expanding her reach across dance-pop, EDM and R&B. It was the moment she moved from promising young singer to chart regular. Dangerous Woman in 2016 sharpened her adult-pop identity. The title track was theatrical in its own way, a slow-burn vocal performance built around control rather than volume alone. Then came the event that changed the emotional context of her career. On May 22, 2017, a terrorist attack at Manchester Arena after Grande’s concert killed 22 people and injured many others. Grande returned to Manchester less than two weeks later for One Love Manchester, a benefit concert that included artists such as Coldplay, Miley Cyrus, Justin Bieber, Katy Perry, Liam Gallagher and others. The concert raised funds for victims and their families and became a defining public moment in her life. It also altered how many people perceived her. She was no longer only a pop star with a devoted fan base; she was a young performer forced to carry grief on an international stage while trying to offer comfort. Her 2018 album Sweetener, produced in part with Pharrell Williams and Max Martin, reflected a search for lightness after trauma. It won the Grammy Award for Best Pop Vocal Album, her first Grammy, and included No Tears Left to Cry, one of her most elegant singles, a song that turned recovery into motion. The following year, thank u, next arrived with startling speed and cultural force. Written amid personal upheaval, including the death of Mac Miller and the end of her engagement to Pete Davidson, the album felt unusually immediate for a mainstream pop release. The title track was not a revenge song but a carefully balanced act of acknowledgement, gratitude and self-preservation. The album made Grande the first artist since the Beatles to occupy the top three positions on the Billboard Hot 100 simultaneously, with 7 rings, break up with your girlfriend, i’m bored and thank u, next. That statistic became a headline, but the deeper achievement was tonal. Grande had found a way to turn tabloid saturation into a controlled pop narrative without sounding swallowed by it. Positions in 2020 leaned into smoother R&B and domestic intimacy, and her duet with Lady Gaga, Rain on Me, won the Grammy for Best Pop Duo/Group Performance. She also served as a coach on The Voice, a role that displayed a more relaxed public version of her, especially in how she spoke to young singers about phrasing, nerves and vocal choices. Her 2024 album eternal sunshine marked another pivot: concise, polished, emotionally self-aware and shaped by the language of memory, image and reinvention. It arrived as the public was already anticipating her return to the screen in Wicked. The casting of Grande as Glinda was one of those entertainment announcements that made instant sense to theater fans and still invited skepticism from others. Glinda is not just a soprano role. She requires comic snap, precise vocal placement and a performer who can make glitter feel like armor. Grande had the background for it, but Wicked was still a major test because film musical acting demands intimacy as much as scale. Her acclaimed performance did more than validate the casting. It reframed her career as a long apprenticeship. The Nickelodeon comic instincts, the Broadway discipline, the pop vocal command and the public understanding of performance all converged in one role.

Why ShowBiz Selected This Entertainer

Grande is featured this week because Wicked has shifted the conversation around her from pop superstardom to screen-musical achievement. Her Glinda performance earned major awards recognition, including Golden Globe and Academy Award nominations, and it arrives at a meaningful point in her career. Rather than functioning as a celebrity casting stunt, Wicked has revealed the continuity in her work: the Broadway teenager, the Nickelodeon comic actor, the technically agile vocalist and the adult pop artist are all visible in the same performance. The awards spotlight matters because it recognizes a skill set that has been present for years but not always taken seriously by audiences outside her fan base. For Grande, Wicked is not a detour. It is a return with higher stakes.
