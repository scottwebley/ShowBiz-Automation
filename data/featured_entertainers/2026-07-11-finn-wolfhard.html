FEATURED ENTERTAINER OF THE WEEK

Finn Wolfhard

Actor and musician

July 11, 2026

Actor/musician Finn Wolfhard planning Phoenix tour stop

Finn Wolfhard has spent most of his public life being recognized before he could reasonably have known what fame was supposed to feel like. He was 13 when Stranger Things turned him into one of the faces of Netflix’s streaming-era takeover, and he was still a teenager when It made him part of one of the highest-grossing horror films ever released. For many young actors, that would have been enough: grow up on a hit series, graduate into franchise films, and let the machine do the rest. Wolfhard has taken a more restless route. He has fronted bands, made guitar-driven records, directed shorts, co-written a feature, and chosen roles that often sit slightly off-center from the obvious teen-star path. That is why this week’s news matters. With his sophomore solo album, Fire From The Hip, released on July 10, and a Phoenix tour stop set for October, Wolfhard is not simply adding music to an acting résumé. He is insisting that music is part of the same creative identity that has shaped his career since Vancouver, YouTube auditions, garage-band rehearsals, and the strange alchemy of adolescence collided in public view.

Early Life

Finn Wolfhard grew up in Vancouver, a city with a film and television infrastructure large enough to make acting feel possible without making Hollywood feel local. His father, Eric Wolfhard, has worked as a researcher on Indigenous land claims in Canada, and his mother, Mary Jolivet, has largely remained outside the public spotlight. His older brother, Nick Wolfhard, became an actor and voice performer, giving the family an unusual two-brother foothold in the entertainment business without the atmosphere of a traditional show-business dynasty. Finn’s entry into acting was modern in a way that now feels almost emblematic of his generation: he found his first acting opportunity through Craigslist. Early screen parts came in short films and guest appearances, including roles in The 100 and Supernatural. They were not star-making jobs, but they gave him what child actors need most and least glamorously: time on set, the ability to take direction, and a sense that acting is work before it is mythology. Music was present early as well. Wolfhard has often been associated with guitar music, and his taste leaned toward alternative and indie rock rather than polished teen-pop branding. That distinction mattered. When fame arrived, he did not sound like someone trying to retrofit a singing career onto television celebrity. He sounded like a young musician who happened to have become extremely famous in another medium.

Career

Wolfhard’s career is a case study in what happens when a young performer lands inside a cultural phenomenon and then refuses to let that phenomenon become the only definition of his talent. Stranger Things premiered on Netflix in 2016 with comparatively modest expectations outside genre circles. Within weeks, it had become something larger: a nostalgia object, a supernatural mystery, a showcase for young actors, and one of the clearest signs that streaming television could create global stars at a speed once reserved for blockbuster movies. As Mike Wheeler, Wolfhard played the emotional anchor of the kids’ ensemble. Mike was not the loudest or strangest character, but he was often the one through whom the audience understood loyalty, first love, grief, and fear. That role required a kind of sincerity that could have curdled in less natural hands. Wolfhard made Mike feel like a real kid living inside extraordinary circumstances, which is harder than it sounds when the circumstances include government labs, interdimensional monsters, and walkie-talkie heroics. The next major turn came with It in 2017. Playing Richie Tozier, Wolfhard moved from streaming sensation to theatrical blockbuster. Richie was fast-talking, profane, anxious, and funny, a role that could have become pure comic relief. Wolfhard gave him comic velocity but also a defensive edge, making the character’s humor feel like armor. The film’s success proved that he could exist outside Hawkins and still hold the screen. It also linked him to a different strand of pop culture: the Stephen King adaptation, the horror coming-of-age story, and the ensemble of young performers whose chemistry carried a major studio release. What is interesting is what Wolfhard did not do next. He did not simply chase the biggest available version of the same part. In The Goldfinch, he played young Boris, a character with a slippery accent, chaotic charm, and a melancholy undertow. The film itself received mixed reviews, but the role showed his willingness to test himself in literary drama. The Turning put him inside gothic horror; Dog Days placed him in a lighter ensemble comedy; The Addams Family and Guillermo del Toro’s Pinocchio moved him into voice performance. Then Ghostbusters: Afterlife gave him another franchise, but one with a different weight. As Trevor, he joined a property with decades of audience affection and the added emotional burden of honoring Harold Ramis. The film leaned on legacy, but Wolfhard’s casting also made sense because he had already become shorthand for a younger generation’s relationship with supernatural adventure. Parallel to the acting career, Wolfhard’s music life has remained unusually consistent. He first became widely known as the frontman and rhythm guitarist for Calpurnia, a Vancouver indie-rock band that released the Scout EP in 2018 on Royal Mountain Records. Calpurnia was notable because it sounded like a band, not a celebrity side project manufactured in a conference room. After that group ended, Wolfhard formed The Aubreys with drummer Malcolm Craig, releasing music that continued his interest in scrappy, melodic, guitar-forward songwriting. His solo work has since sharpened the idea that he is not visiting music between acting jobs; he is building a second body of work alongside the first. His move behind the camera is another important piece. Wolfhard directed the short film Night Shifts and later co-wrote and co-directed the slasher-comedy feature Hell of a Summer with Billy Bryk. That leap is meaningful because horror has been one of the defining genres of his screen life. Instead of merely appearing in other people’s horror stories, he began shaping his own. The result is a career that looks less like a straight line than a set of overlapping obsessions: adolescence, fear, friendship, jokes made under pressure, analog textures, guitar noise, and the complicated process of growing up while millions of people watch.

Why ShowBiz Selected This Entertainer

Wolfhard is featured this week because Fire From The Hip and the accompanying tour news catch him at a revealing point in his career. The Phoenix date is not just another stop on a concert calendar; it is evidence of a performer deliberately building an adult creative identity in public. Many viewers first encountered him as a kid on bicycles in Stranger Things or as Richie in It, but his music career has become one of the clearest signs that he does not see acting and music as separate personas. The album release gives fresh momentum to that second track while the tour brings him face to face with audiences outside the screen franchises that made him famous. That connection is the larger story: Wolfhard’s career has always been about the overlap between fandom, genre culture, youth, anxiety, humor, and noise. Fire From The Hip arrives as he continues to carry the weight of Stranger Things, Ghostbusters, and his own filmmaking ambitions, making this a week when the musician and the actor are unusually easy to see in the same frame.
